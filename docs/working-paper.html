Who Should Use Singapore's Supplementary Retirement Scheme — and Who Shouldn't?

A Lifecycle and Tax-Arbitrage Analysis of Singapore's Supplementary Retirement Scheme: Indifference Curves and Optimal Participation Regions

NTU URECA Project NBS26004
Nanyang Business School, Nanyang Technological University
Draft of 16 November 2026

Abstract

Singapore's Supplementary Retirement Scheme (SRS) gives savers a tax deduction today in exchange for locking money up until retirement age, after which only half of each withdrawal is taxed. Despite SRS holding S$23.88 billion across 516,376 accounts (MOF, Dec 2025), no peer-reviewed study has taken it as a primary subject. This paper builds three linked models — a closed-form per-dollar condition, a Monte-Carlo drawdown simulation, and a 50-year projection calibrated to Singapore's actual historical tax schedules (YA2003–2024) — to map who benefits from SRS, by how much, and when contributing more stops paying off. The central result is a single inequality, tc > 0.5·tw, which governs the entire participation decision independently of investment return or horizon, together with a hard ceiling: the effective tax rate on a qualifying withdrawal can never exceed 12%.

1.Introduction: An Unstudied Scheme

SRS was introduced in 2001 and is operated through three agent banks — DBS/POSB, OCBC and UOB. Despite its size, a systematic search across Google Scholar, SSRN, RePEc/IDEAS, and the institutional repositories of NUS, SMU and NTU found no peer-reviewed journal article, working paper, thesis, or dissertation that takes SRS as its primary subject (literature review, Stream 1). SRS appears only in passing inside broader studies of Singapore's compulsory CPF scheme, or in the OECD's regional pensions report — and Singapore is absent from the OECD's main comparison tables altogether, since it is not a member country.

The Ministry of Finance has itself acknowledged the gap, stating it "will continue to study this matter, together with academics and researchers" (MOF Parliamentary Reply, 2019). The same reply gives the best available snapshot of who actually uses the scheme: of people who contributed in a given year, 90% have assessable income above S$80,000, contribute an average of about S$14,000 a year, and face an average marginal tax rate of roughly 15%; the remaining 10% contribute about S$9,000 on average at a much lower ~3.2% marginal rate.

2.Institutional Setting

Every rule below is sourced to IRAS or MOF directly (full inline citations in docs/srs-rules.md of the project repository). Four features define the trade:

  1. The deduction going in. Each dollar contributed reduces chargeable income, up to an annual cap of S$15,300 for citizens/PRs (S$35,700 for foreigners), within the S$80,000 overall personal relief cap.
  2. The age lock. Penalty-free withdrawal starts at the statutory retirement age in force when the saver made their first contribution — 62 before July 2022, 63 to June 2026, 64 thereafter, with 65 announced by 2030. A single S$1 contribution locks in the prevailing age for life.
  3. The concession coming out. From the locked-in age, only 50% of each withdrawal is taxable at ordinary resident rates, over a 10-year window that starts at the first withdrawal. With no other income, up to S$40,000 a year (S$400,000 over ten years) is entirely tax-free.
  4. The penalty for early exit. Withdrawing before the locked-in age makes 100% of the amount taxable plus a 5% penalty, with exceptions for medical incapacity, terminal illness, death, and bankruptcy.

3.Methodology: Three Linked Models

The project builds three computational models, each relaxing an assumption of the one before it. Every equation below is implemented exactly as shown in the code (docs/methodology-equations-assumptions.md) — none are simplified for presentation. Notation: tc is the relief rate at contribution; tw the marginal tax rate at withdrawal; I = 0.5 the taxable share of a qualifying withdrawal; r the annual return; n the horizon in years.

3.1 Model 1 — the per-dollar core (srs_model.py)

For one pre-tax dollar at a fixed return and horizon, is SRS or an ordinary account larger at the end? Because Singapore has no capital gains tax and exempts one-tier dividends for individuals, the ordinary account carries zero tax drag. Tax on chargeable income x is the usual band sum,

tax(x) = Σi (min(x, Ui) − Ui−1) · ratei (1)

where Ui are the IRAS band edges (YA2026 schedule).

and the terminal-wealth ratio of the SRS route to the ordinary route, per dollar, is

k(tc, tw) = 1 − I·tw1 − tc, (2)
SRS wins  ⇔  tc > I·tw. (3)

Note that r and n cancel entirely — the participation decision is independent of return and horizon.

Because progressive brackets make tw a function of the accumulated pot, the pot size at which the next contributed dollar stops paying off has the closed form

pot* = 2(L − other) · a(n, r),   a(n, r) = 1 − (1 + r)−nr, (4)

where L is the lower edge of the first tax band whose rate exceeds 2·tc and a(n,r) is the n-year annuity factor.

3.2 Model 2 — Monte Carlo and dynamic programming (srs_simulation.py)

Model 2 relaxes the fixed-return assumption. Return paths are either i.i.d. lognormal or block-bootstrapped from real S&P 500 history (1928–2025) in five-year blocks, which preserves the clustering of good and bad years that single-year draws erase. The withdrawal policy is solved by backward dynamic programming on the balance grid:

Vk(b) = minw { tax(I·w)·(1 + r)k−1 + Vk−1(b(1 + r) − w) }, (5)

with terminal condition V1(b) = tax(I·b(1+r)) — the deemed withdrawal of whatever remains at the end of the 10-year window.

The early-exit decision is modelled as a real option: outside opportunities arrive as a Poisson process and each must clear a breakeven excess return,

x*excess = x* − rforecast,   Nyear ∼ Poisson(λ),   X ∼ Exp(mean). (6)

where x* is the absolute breakeven return for breaking the lock-up now rather than waiting for the penalty-free unlock.

3.3 Model 3 — the real tax-regime history, projected (srs_tax_regime.py)

Model 3 replaces the stationary-tax assumption with the six actual IRAS resident-rate schedules (YA2003, 2006, 2007, 2012, 2017, 2024) and projects 50 years forward two ways, calibrated to the same total drift so the comparison isolates how changes arrive rather than how large they are:

discrete:   shift(y) = avg_step · ⌊y / avg_interval⌋, (7)
smooth:   shift(y) = drift_per_year · y, (8)

calibrated from history: avg_step ≈ +1.33 pp per revision, avg_interval ≈ 5.7 years, drift ≈ +0.235 pp/year.

4.Results

4.1 The entire decision is one inequality

Since both routes grow at the identical rate for the same number of years, the comparison collapses to eq. (3). Numerically, with the exit rate held at tw = 7%:

SRS terminal wealth = (1 + r)n × (1 − 0.5·tw)
Ordinary account  = (1 + r)n × (1 − tc)
SRS wins ⇔ tc > 0.5 × tw — for every r and every n.

4.3 Why higher returns shrink the percentage edge

The tax-free half of a withdrawal is a fixed exemption, but as returns rise, a larger share of the final pot is investment growth rather than contributions. The dollar advantage rises with return while the percentage advantage falls, because the exempt half covers a shrinking fraction of a faster-growing pot.

4.4 The participation ceiling — and why it only exists since 2024

Progressive brackets mean a bigger pot pushes the taxable half of each withdrawal into higher bands, so the frontier of "is the next dollar still worth contributing" is a staircase: flat across each tax band, stepping up at each band edge. Applying eq. (4) gives the exact crossover pot for each relief bracket:

The 12% figure is a structural cap: top rate 24% × 50% inclusion. The average exit rate approaches but never reaches it.

Model 3 adds the historical dimension: this ceiling did not exist before 2024. Under every schedule from YA2007 to YA2023 the top rate (20–22%) never exceeded 2 × 11.5% = 23%, so there was no pot size at which an 11.5%-bracket saver should stop contributing. The YA2024 reform, which raised the top rate to 24%, created the plateau for the first time — at exactly the S$15,443,470 the closed form gives today.

4.5 The fixed-$40,000 heuristic underperforms level drawdown

A popular shortcut says "withdraw exactly S$40,000 a year — it's tax-free." True, but it fully drains only a pot of up to S$308,869; above that, the remainder is deemed withdrawn in one lump at the end of the ten-year window and taxed all at once. On the base-case S$953,537 pot, level drawdown pays S$20,721 of total tax against S$89,911 under the S$40k rule — the rule of thumb costs S$69,190 (docs/findings.md §9).

4.6 Breaking the lock-up early: a real-option hurdle

An early withdrawal loses the 50% concession (100% becomes taxable) and adds a 5% penalty — an extra cost of 0.05 + 0.5τ over a qualifying withdrawal, between 8.5% and 17% depending on bracket. 4.7 Idle cash is the biggest avoidable loss

Contributions sit as cash earning 0.05% a year until actively invested. The same 29-year stream that reaches S$953,537 at 5% ends at just S$446,820 if left in cash — a gap of roughly half a million dollars, about ten times the entire lifetime tax relief earned (29 × S$1,759.50 ≈ S$51,026). This matches the real-world figure that about 21% of all SRS money (≈S$5.0 billion) sits uninvested.

"Residual" is whatever is still in the account when the 10-year window closes, deemed withdrawn and taxed in Year 10 under every strategy — it is already included in the total tax column, not a separate leftover.

The $40,000 rule is the clear loser, by a wide margin, at every income level. Because it withdraws the same trickle regardless of how large the pot has grown — and under this path's strong late-decade returns the pot swells past S$2.9M — it leaves S$984,772 sitting until the window closes, where it is deemed withdrawn in one lump and taxed at roughly 1.5× to over 7× what either level drawdown or DP pays, depending on how much other income already fills the cheap tax bands.

Between the two re-planned strategies, the result is closer than headline figures suggest, and it is not simply "DP wins." At every income level here, dynamic programming actually pays slightly more nominal tax than the much simpler level-drawdown re-plan (S$11,595 vs S$11,558 at $0 other income, widening to S$94,543 vs S$88,498 at $160k). That is not a bug: the DP solver minimises future-value-compounded tax (eq. 5), not the plain nominal sum, so it will accept a dollar of extra nominal tax paid later in exchange for a smaller present-value burden — and part of the gap is simply the known numerical noise of its discretised balance grid (Section 6.5, Table 6). The practical conclusion is reassuring rather than dramatic: a much simpler, closed-form level-drawdown re-plan already captures almost all of the benefit a full dynamic program offers, as long as it is actually re-planned every year from the real balance — which is exactly what the $40,000 rule refuses to do.

Figure 7b shows level drawdown and DP tracking each other closely under real market volatility. It's worth checking the control case directly: what does each strategy do if the market instead delivers exactly the 5% forecast, every single year, with no surprises at all?

7.Contribution to the Literature

The theory this project builds on is mature — Dammon, Spatt & Zhang (2004, Journal of Finance) on asset location across taxable and tax-deferred accounts; Horan (2006, Financial Analysts Journal) on withdrawal under progressive rates; Brown, Cederburg & O'Doherty (2017, Journal of Financial Economics) on saving under tax-rate uncertainty (literature review, Streams 3, 5, 9). Three gaps emerge that no existing paper fills:

  • The no-capital-gains-tax decomposition. Prior work assumes the ordinary account is taxed on growth — the main reason to shelter investments at all. Singapore taxes neither capital gains nor (for individuals) dividends, so that channel is switched off; what remains is pure rate arbitrage between tc and tw, isolated cleanly for the first time.
  • A flat, fractional (50%) inclusion rate. The OECD's EET/TEE taxonomy of retirement schemes has no clean cell for taxing a flat half of every withdrawal at ordinary rates. India's NPS (60% exempt) and the UK's 25% tax-free lump sum are partial analogues, but both work differently.
  • A finite forced-liquidation window, not a minimum-distribution floor. US accounts impose required minimum distributions — a floor. SRS instead gives exactly ten years from the first withdrawal, after which the remainder is deemed withdrawn in one shot. Planning backwards from a hard deadline is a different optimisation problem, and it is unmodelled in the withdrawal-sequencing literature.

Separately from the academic record, it is worth checking whether the lay argument "don't put too much into SRS — you'll just pay more tax" circulates in practice. It does, in three practitioner sources (archived with screenshots in docs/literature-screenshots/): a Seedly thread, "Does SRS ever not make sense?" (2019), where commenters reach an informal version of the breakeven condition ("contribute only if you are at least in the 7% tax bracket"); Investment Moats, "Is it Always Better to Contribute to SRS Account?", which notes the edge is small for low brackets precisely because Singapore's ordinary accounts are already untaxed; and a HardwareZone Forums thread, "Is it a mistake to overly contribute into SRS?", found but unverified behind an age-verification wall. None are citable as evidence of fact — they are evidence the intuition exists, which the participation frontier of Figure 3 formalises.

8.Limitations

All results assume: nominal Singapore dollars with no inflation adjustment (supported by Model 3's finding that band thresholds have been frozen in nominal terms for 21+ years); no fees in either account; no foreign-dividend withholding tax on the ordinary account; the S$80,000 overall personal relief cap is not modelled; and no mortality risk during drawdown. The Monte-Carlo return series is single-country US history (S&P 500, 1928–2025) as an approved stand-in, not calibrated to SRS-eligible Singapore instruments. The early-exit arrival rate λ and excess-return distribution are illustrative placeholders, so the shape of Figure 5 — not any single headline number from the Poisson simulation — is the finding.
