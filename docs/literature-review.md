# Literature Review — *A Lifecycle and Tax-Arbitrage Analysis of Singapore's Supplementary Retirement Scheme: Indifference Curves and Optimal Participation Regions*
### Prepared for NTU URECA Project NBS26004 (Assoc Prof Nelson Lau, Nanyang Business School) — supervisor meeting, 25 September 2026

> **Referencing convention:** every claim carries its source URL/citation inline, per supervisor instruction. Where a source could not be accessed or the evidence is thin, this is flagged explicitly.

---

## TL;DR
- **There is no peer-reviewed academic study taking the SRS as its primary subject.** This was verified across Google Scholar, SSRN, RePEc/IDEAS, the NUS/SMU/NTU institutional repositories, and the leading pension journals. The absence is genuine and defensibly stateable in a paper — making the project's core novelty strong.
- **The theoretical scaffolding the project relies on is mature and highly cited** (Dammon–Spatt–Zhang on asset location; Horan and Reichenstein on after-tax valuation; Brown–Cederburg–O'Doherty on tax-rate uncertainty; Beshears et al. on optimal illiquidity). The project therefore sits on solid theoretical ground while addressing an entirely unstudied institutional case.
- **The two most distinctive, publishable contributions available are** (a) the **flat fractional (50%) inclusion regime**, which has no clean cell in the OECD EET/TEE taxonomy, and (b) the **no-capital-gains-tax decomposition** that isolates pure rate arbitrage from the tax-shelter-on-growth channel. Both are real gaps; the finite forced-liquidation withdrawal window is a third.

---

## Key Findings
1. **SRS academic void — established (genuinely absent).** No primary-subject peer-reviewed work exists; only passing mentions inside CPF/retirement-adequacy studies and grey literature.
2. **CPF is well-studied — established.** It supplies the adjacent, tax-exempt scheme whose payouts interact with SRS withdrawal taxation.
3. **Tax-deferred-vs-taxable theory — established and directly portable**, but built around a tax-shelter-on-growth channel that is near-worthless in Singapore.
4. **Withdrawal-sequencing/decumulation methods — deep but US-tax-code specific**; dynamic programming and terminal-window treatments are thin.
5. **Tax-rate uncertainty — one canonical paper (BCO 2017) with a growing citing set**; "tax diversification across wrappers" maps onto SRS/CPF/taxable choice.
6. **Illiquidity/penalty theory — established**, giving a precise ~10% social-optimum benchmark against the project's 8.5–16% effective SRS penalty.
7. **Fractional-inclusion regimes — contested/under-studied**; India and the UK are partial analogues but structurally different, so Singapore's design is distinctive with nuance.
8. **No-CGT decomposition — genuinely absent.** No paper decomposes deferred-account value into rate-arbitrage vs shelter-on-growth components for a no-CGT jurisdiction.
9. **Indifference/participation-region methodology — partially established** (breakeven-tax-rate papers), but a full participation-region map with progressive-bracket plateaus is not in the literature.
10. **Behavioural defaults/idle cash — established in general, absent for SRS specifically.**

## Details

### Stream 1 — Singapore SRS: exhaustive search for academic work *(established: genuinely absent)*
The prior web-only finding is **confirmed and strengthened**. No peer-reviewed journal article, working paper, thesis, or dissertation takes the SRS as its primary subject. The only SRS-dedicated, scholar-indexed item is Kung Wai Ming (2001), "Singapore introduces its Supplementary Retirement Scheme to encourage retirement savings," *International Tax Review* 12(4):60 (https://www.proquest.com/openview/e2b246b02df59cd771560a0da388acdb/1) — but this is a one-page practitioner trade-journal note, **not** peer-reviewed research. SRS otherwise appears only in passing: within CPF-centric scholarship such as McCarthy, Mitchell & Piggott (2002) (see Stream 2); in the OECD *Pensions at a Glance Asia/Pacific 2024* Singapore country profile (https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-asia-pacific-2024_d4146d12-en/full-report/singapore_b2e9a32c.html); and in an SMU InK tax-law text referencing SRS withholding provisions (Income Tax Act ss. 45E/45EA) (https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=4714&context=sol_research).

Databases searched that returned **no** SRS-primary result: SSRN, RePEc/IDEAS, NUS ScholarBank, SMU InK, NTU DR-NTU, *Singapore Economic Review*, and *Journal of Pension Economics & Finance*. **Caveat:** some content sits behind paywalls (ProQuest, Cambridge Core, JSTOR), so certainty is high but not provably exhaustive. Two SRS-mentioning outlets — David Publishing and IISTE's *Research Journal of Finance and Accounting* — are widely regarded as predatory/low-quality and **must not be cited**.

Institutional statistics for background (all from the Ministry of Finance): total SRS contributions reached **S$23.88 billion by December 2025, up 16.0% year-on-year**, with account holders rising from 221,849 (Dec 2020) to **516,376 (Dec 2025)** (MOF cumulative statistics, reported via SmartWealth, https://www.smartwealth.sg/). On the contributor profile, a MOF Parliamentary Reply by then-Minister for Finance Heng Swee Keat (2019) states that **90% of those who made an SRS contribution in the past year have Assessable Income above S$80,000, an average marginal tax rate of about 15.0%, and an average contribution of about S$14,000**; the remaining 10% have Assessable Income below S$80,000, an average contribution of about S$9,000, and an average marginal tax rate of about 3.2% (https://www.mof.gov.sg/news-resources/newsroom/data-on-supplementary-retirement-scheme-for-the-last-5-years/). MOF has itself stated it "will continue to study this matter, together with academics and researchers," implying such research is genuinely nascent (same source). Note this profile — a majority of contributors facing ~15% marginal rates — is materially relevant to the project's tc > 0.5·tw participation condition.

Scheme mechanics for the model (IRAS/MOF/CPF Board): contributions are tax-deductible up to the cap (S$15,300 citizens/PRs, S$35,700 foreigners) within the S$80,000 personal relief cap; investment returns accrue tax-free except Singapore dividends; only **50% of withdrawals on/after the prescribed retirement age are taxable**; premature withdrawals are **fully taxable plus a 5% penalty**; withdrawals can be spread over a 10-year window; up to S$400,000 is tax-exempt on death/terminal illness (https://www.iras.gov.sg/taxes/individual-income-tax/basics-of-individual-income-tax/special-tax-schemes/srs-contributions; https://www.mof.gov.sg/news-resources/supplementary-retirement-scheme/).

### Stream 2 — Singapore CPF research *(well studied)*
The CPF is Singapore's compulsory retirement savings system, and unlike SRS, academics have written about it a fair amount. That makes it the natural neighbour to cite.

Most people start with McCarthy, Mitchell & Piggott (2002), "Asset rich and cash poor in Singapore? Retirement provision in a national defined contribution pension fund," *Journal of Pension Economics & Finance* 1(3) (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-pension-economics-and-finance/issue/7071AE88CC86A14B90F75B954A7B71DF). The title gives the finding away. Because CPF money can be used to buy a home, many Singaporeans retire with a lot of wealth tied up in property and not much cash to live on.

Two papers ask whether CPF actually produces enough income in retirement. Chia & Tsui (2003), "Life annuities of compulsory savings and income adequacy of the elderly in Singapore," *JPEF* 2(1):41–65, is the older one. The newer one, "Mandatory annuitization and money's worth: evidence from Singapore," checks whether CPF LIFE pays out fairly for what members put in (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-pension-economics-and-finance/article/mandatory-annuitization-and-moneys-worth-evidence-from-singapore/0176186DB7E33CAEB2997231B699F014). "Money's worth" just means: for each dollar you pay in, how many dollars of expected payout do you get back. That paper was funded by an MOE Tier-1 grant at NUS.

If you read one overview, make it Koh's "Singapore's Social Security Savings System" (Pension Research Council, WP2014-18), which covers the whole system (https://pensionresearchcouncil.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/WP2014-18-Koh.pdf). Koh also notes that many members leave money sitting in low-interest accounts because of transaction costs, weak financial literacy and plain inertia. That is the same behaviour you see with idle SRS cash in Stream 10. For the local policy argument about whether CPF is enough, the Institute of Policy Studies has a readable summary (https://lkyspp.nus.edu.sg/docs/default-source/ips/1-the-debate-on-cpf-and-retirement-adequacy-and-the-ips-forum_4_v4.pdf).

The one fact from this stream your model relies on is that CPF LIFE payouts are not taxed (OECD Singapore profile, https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-asia-pacific-2024_d4146d12-en/full-report/singapore_b2e9a32c.html). So a retiree's CPF income doesn't push their SRS withdrawals into a higher bracket, and SRS is often their only taxable income. That's a big part of why the exit tax rate comes out so low. It's also why tw depends on the retiree's whole income picture rather than on SRS alone.

### Stream 3 — Taxable versus tax-deferred accounts: the core theory *(well studied)*
This is the main body of theory the project sits on. The question these papers ask is simple to state. If you have a normal taxable investment account and a tax-deferred retirement account, which investments should go in which?

The paper everyone cites is Dammon, Spatt & Zhang (2004), "Optimal Asset Location and Allocation with Taxable and Tax-Deferred Investing," *Journal of Finance* 59(3):999–1037 (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2004.00655.x; free PDF: https://personal.utdallas.edu/~hxz054000/JOFI_2.pdf). It won the TIAA-CREF Paul A. Samuelson Award (https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/spch010705cs-remarks-presentation-paul-samuelson-award-publication-lifetime-financial-security-tiaa-cref). Their answer, roughly: keep bonds in the retirement account and stocks in the taxable one. Bond interest is taxed as income every year, so sheltering it saves a lot. Stock gains are only taxed when you sell, and at a lower capital gains rate, so stocks are already fairly tax-efficient in a normal account. Their earlier paper, Dammon, Spatt & Zhang (2001), "Optimal Consumption and Investment with Capital Gains Taxes," *Review of Financial Studies* 14:583–616, builds the underlying model.

Other names you'll run into: Shoven & Sialm, and Poterba, Shoven & Sialm (2000), who work on the same "which account holds what" question. Vanguard's summary is easier going than the papers (https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/revisiting_conventional_wisdom_regarding_asset_location.pdf). Horan (2005), *Tax-Advantaged Savings Accounts and Tax-Efficient Wealth Accumulation* (Research Foundation of CFA Institute), is a book-length treatment and probably the best single thing to read first. Reichenstein's work, including Reichenstein, Horan & Jennings, "Two Key Concepts for Wealth Management and Beyond," *Financial Analysts Journal* (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2469/faj.v68.n1.2), makes a point worth remembering: money in a pre-tax account isn't entirely yours, because part of it is the government's future tax bill, so you should value it after tax. Huang (2001) and Garlappi & Huang (2006), "Are Stocks Desirable in Tax-Deferred Accounts?", push the same logic further. For something recent, Das et al. (2021), "Combining Investment and Tax Strategies for Optimizing Lifetime Solvency under Uncertain Returns and Mortality," *JRFM* 14(7):285, handles the investment and tax decisions together (https://doi.org/10.3390/jrfm14070285).

Almost all of this work assumes investment gains get taxed outside the retirement account. That assumption is the whole reason to shelter bonds or worry about which account holds what. Singapore doesn't tax capital gains and doesn't tax dividends from Singapore companies, so most of that worry disappears. What remains is only the difference between the tax rate you save when you contribute and the rate you pay when you withdraw. In effect, the project takes this well-known model and studies the case where one of its two moving parts has been switched off.

### Stream 4 — Withdrawal order and drawing down in retirement *(well studied, mostly for the US)*
Once you've retired with money spread across several accounts, what order should you take it out in, and how much each year, to pay the least tax? That's what these papers work on.

The starting point is Ragsdale, Seila & Little (1994), "An Optimization Model for Scheduling Withdrawals from Tax-Deferred Retirement Accounts," *Financial Services Review* 3(2):93–108 (https://openjournals.libs.uga.edu/fsr/article/download/3726/3208/10776). They set it up as a linear programming problem (the same kind of optimisation you meet in an operations research course) and built in early withdrawal penalties and minimum withdrawal rules. Spitzer & Singh (2006), "Extending Retirement Payouts by Optimizing the Sequence of Withdrawals," *Journal of Financial Planning* 19(4):52–61, assume a single flat tax rate and find you should use up the account with the lowest expected return first. Coopersmith & Sumutka, and Sumutka, Sumutka & Coopersmith (2012), put much more realistic tax calculations into the same problem. DiLellio & Ostrov (*Decision Sciences* 48:356–384, 2017, with later papers in *Financial Services Review*) work out exactly when to switch from one account to the next, and estimate that the common rules of thumb fall 10–26% short of the best plan (https://tme.journals.libs.uga.edu/fsr/article/view/3419).

On methods, which matters for the dynamic programming idea from the meeting: Das et al. (2021) optimise tax and investment decisions together while letting returns be random. A 2025 arXiv paper uses Model Predictive Control, a technique from engineering that re-plans every period as new information arrives, and reports that it leaves more money behind than the standard "4% rule" in about 70% of simulations (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10603). That one is a preprint and hasn't been peer-reviewed.

What none of these papers handle is a deadline. US retirement accounts have "required minimum distributions", which force you to take out at least a certain amount each year past a certain age. That's a floor. SRS works the other way. You get ten years from your first withdrawal, and whatever is still in the account at the end is treated as withdrawn in one go. Planning backwards from a fixed end date is a different problem, and nobody has modelled it.

### Stream 5 — Not knowing future tax rates *(one key paper)*
Your model assumes today's tax brackets stay put for forty years. They won't, and this stream is about deciding what to do when you can't know future tax rates.

The paper to read is Brown, Cederburg & O'Doherty (2017), "Tax Uncertainty and Retirement Savings Diversification," *Journal of Financial Economics* 126(3):689–712 (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X17302519; free version: https://repository.arizona.edu/bitstream/handle/10150/627841/RothTaxUncertainty_CopyEditVersion.pdf). It won the 2018 TIAA Samuelson Award (https://business.missouri.edu/about/news/research-tax-uncertainty-and-savings-diversification-wins-tiaa-paul-samuelson-award). The US has two main kinds of retirement account: traditional ones (tax break now, taxed on the way out) and Roth ones (taxed now, tax-free on the way out). Since nobody knows what tax rates will be decades from now, they find most people should hold some of each, much as you'd spread investments rather than bet everything on one outcome. They build on the 2001 Dammon–Spatt–Zhang model from Stream 3 and on Lachance (2013), who looked at the same choice under progressive taxes.

A Singaporean choosing between SRS, CPF and an ordinary investment account is making the same kind of bet on future tax rates, and the schedule does move: Singapore raised its top rate to 24% from YA2024. This is where the "tax rate creep" input from the meeting belongs. One difference is that Singapore has no Roth-style account. The ordinary investment account is the only alternative, and it's already close to tax-free because there's no capital gains tax.

### Stream 6 — Lock-ups, early withdrawal penalties and money leaking out *(well studied; one gap)*
Why lock retirement money up at all, and how big should the penalty for early withdrawal be? The main paper on this is Beshears, Choi, Clayton, Harris, Laibson & Madrian, "Optimal Illiquidity," published in the *Journal of Financial Economics* in 2025. It first came out as NBER Working Paper 27459 in 2020 (https://www.nber.org/papers/w27459; journal version: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X25000042).

It rests on "present bias": people weight money now more heavily than their future selves would like, so they dip into savings too early. Locking money up protects you from yourself, but you also need some access for genuine emergencies. Balancing the two, they find the best setup is roughly three accounts: one you can use freely, one you can't touch at all, and one with about a 10% penalty for early withdrawal, which is close to what the US already charges on 401(k)s and IRAs. If everyone had the same degree of present bias, written β (where β = 1 means no bias), the best penalty would be about 1 − β. The theory underneath is Amador, Werning & Angeletos (2006) on the trade-off between commitment and flexibility, which is worth reading directly rather than through other people's summaries.

That gives you a yardstick for Singapore. The SRS penalty looks like 5%, but withdrawing early also loses you the 50% tax concession, so the real extra cost is about 5% plus half your tax rate. That works out at roughly 8.5% for someone in the 7% bracket and 16% in the 22% bracket, so it sits around their ~10% figure.

On money leaving retirement accounts early, usually called "leakage", see Argento, Bryant & Sabelhaus (2015), "Early Withdrawals from Retirement Accounts During the Great Recession," *Contemporary Economic Policy* 33(1):1–16 (https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/coecpo/v33y2015i1p1-16.html). Their Federal Reserve working paper (FEDS 2013-22, https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2013/201322/) found that in 2010, for every dollar US households under 55 put into retirement accounts, roughly 41 cents came back out early: about 20 cents with the penalty paid and 21 cents where the penalty was waived. Beshears et al. (2015), "Liquidity in Retirement Savings Systems: An International Comparison," *AER* 105(5):420–425, compares how different countries handle early access.

Nobody has yet modelled the side-question from the meeting. Picture an investor with money locked in SRS who gets offered good outside opportunities (private equity, property, a business) at random times and has to decide whether paying to break the lock-up is worth it. The closest papers are a 2023 *JFE* paper on commitment and early withdrawal penalties (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S030440682300037X) and "Optimal Retirement Saving and Dissaving" in *JFQA* (https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/optimal-retirement-saving-and-dissaving/50B4010F5DCF8F6E8873D8D4733FC960). Neither covers it. SRS makes the problem easier to model than most schemes, because the cost of breaking the lock-up is a known number that tops out at about 17% (5% plus half of the 24% top rate).

### Stream 7 — How other countries tax retirement savings *(little written; this is where SRS looks unusual)*
Economists label retirement schemes with three letters, one for each stage: paying in, growing, and taking out. E means exempt and T means taxed. "EET" therefore means no tax going in, no tax while the money grows, and tax on the way out, which is the most common setup in the world. The standard references are the OECD's *Financial Incentives and Retirement Savings* (2018) (https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2018/12/financial-incentives-and-retirement-savings_g1g96ed9/9789264306929-en.pdf) and Yoo & De Serres (2004), OECD Economics Department Working Paper 406. Singapore isn't an OECD member, so it isn't in their data at all. The Tax Foundation has a quick comparison across OECD countries (https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/global/savings-and-investment-oecd/).

SRS doesn't fit the letters neatly. It's E going in and E while growing, but only half-T coming out. The nearest things elsewhere:

- **India's National Pension System.** At retirement you can take 60% of the pot tax-free under Section 10(12A) of the Income Tax Act, but the other 40% has to buy an annuity, and that annuity income is taxed as it's paid (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Pension_System; https://cleartax.in/s/nps-national-pension-scheme). Rule changes in late 2025 let people take up to 80% as a lump sum, but only 60% is tax-free, so the extra 20% gets taxed (https://1finance.co.in/blog/new-nps-withdrawal-rules-taxation/). The idea is similar to SRS, but it comes tied to a compulsory annuity.
- **Australia's superannuation.** Contributions are taxed at a flat 15%, earnings in the fund at up to 15% (10% on long-held assets), and withdrawals are generally tax-free after age 60 (https://treasury.gov.au/programs-and-initiatives-superannuation/distributional-analysis-of-superannuation-taxation-concessions; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation_of_superannuation_in_Australia). That's a lower rate applied throughout rather than taxing a fraction of each withdrawal.
- **The UK's tax-free lump sum.** You can take 25% of your pension tax-free, capped since April 2024 at £268,275, and the rest is taxed as ordinary income (https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/pensions-and-retirement/tax-and-pensions/lump-sum-allowances-for-pensions; https://www.lgpsmember.org/your-pension/the-essentials/tax/lump-sum-limits/). Only the lump sum gets the break, not the withdrawals after it.
- Still to look at properly: Canada's RRSP (EET) and TFSA (TEE), Hong Kong's MPF, Malaysia's EPF and PRS, Thailand's RMF and SSF, Japan's iDeCo and NISA, plus South Korea, Chile and some European schemes.

Half of every withdrawal taxed at your normal rate, with no requirement to buy an annuity: on what's been found so far, that combination does look particular to Singapore. India and the UK are close in spirit but work differently, so the fair claim is that partial tax breaks exist elsewhere, just not in this form.

### Stream 8 — Countries with no capital gains tax *(nothing written yet)*
This stream is short because there's almost nothing in it.

Singapore doesn't tax capital gains, and dividends from Singapore companies are tax-free for individuals, so an ordinary investment account already grows almost tax-free. One of the two usual benefits of a retirement account, protecting your investment growth from tax, is therefore worth close to nothing here. The only benefit left is the gap between the tax rate you save at when you contribute and the rate you pay when you withdraw.

The search found no academic paper that studies retirement accounts in countries without a capital gains tax (Singapore, Hong Kong, New Zealand, and partly Switzerland, the UAE and Malaysia). It also found none that splits a retirement account's value into those two parts, the tax-rate difference and the sheltered growth. The nearest pieces are practitioner and legal writing on what tax deferral is worth: the Financial Planning Association's "Tax Deferral: When Does It Make Sense and When Does It Cost Cents (or Dollars)?" (https://www.financialplanningassociation.org/article/journal/SEP12-tax-deferral-when-does-it-make-sense-and-when-does-it-cost-cents-or-dollars) and "The Real Value of Tax Deferral" in the *Florida Law Review* (https://scholarship.law.ufl.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1676&context=flr). Neither does the formal split, and neither looks at a country without capital gains tax.

This is probably the project's strongest single contribution: taking the well-known model from Stream 3 and working out what happens to it when one of its two parts drops out completely.

### Stream 9 — Breakeven analysis and participation regions *(partly done; the full map is new)*
The project's end product is a map of who should use SRS and who shouldn't. The papers in this stream present their results the same way, as breakeven points.

The clearest example is Krishnan & Cumbie (2016), "Roth Conversion: An Analysis Using Breakeven Tax Rates, Breakeven Periods, and Random Returns," *Journal of Personal Finance* 15(1):37–46. They ask when converting a US traditional account into a Roth pays off, and answer with the tax rate or holding period at which you'd be indifferent, which is the same kind of answer you're producing. In the same style: McQuarrie & DiLellio (2023), "The Arithmetic of Roth Conversions," *Journal of Financial Planning* 36(5):72–89 (https://www.financialplanningassociation.org/learning/publications/journal/MAY23-arithmetic-roth-conversions-OPEN), which is a good model for laying breakeven arithmetic out clearly; DiLellio, Goldfeder & McQuarrie (2023), "Optimal decisions under price dynamics for Roth conversions," *Financial Planning Review* (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/cfp2.1174), which adds random price movements; Horan, Peterson & McLeod (1997); and Horan (2004), "Breakeven holding periods for tax advantaged savings accounts with early withdrawal penalties," *Financial Services Review* 13, which is useful for the early withdrawal section.

The closest match to what you're doing is Horan (2006), "Withdrawal Location with Progressive Tax Rates," *Financial Analysts Journal* 62(6):77–87 (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2469/faj.v62.n6.4355). He shows that under progressive taxes, the best bracket to withdraw into rises the wealthier you are. That's the same effect behind your finding that a bigger SRS pot pushes up the tax rate on the way out.

What hasn't been published is the full map across both tax rates, the one you save at and the one you pay at, including the flat stretches that tax brackets create. Singapore's brackets are wide, so the line between "use SRS" and "don't" isn't a smooth curve. It's a staircase that stays flat across a bracket and then jumps at the edge. Drawing that properly is the third gap.

### Stream 10 — Defaults and money left in cash *(well studied in general; nothing on SRS)*
People tend to stick with whatever option is pre-selected for them, even when switching would clearly pay. The founding paper is Madrian & Shea (2001), "The Power of Suggestion: Inertia in 401(k) Participation and Savings Behavior," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 116(4):1149–1187 (https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/116/4/1149/1903159; free working paper version: https://www.nber.org/papers/w7682). When a US company started enrolling workers into its retirement plan automatically, participation jumped, and most people never touched the defaults. In the working paper version, about 75% stayed at the default 3% contribution rate and about 80% stayed in the default money market fund. Benartzi & Thaler and Choi et al. have built a large literature on the same "power of defaults" idea.

SRS has the reverse problem: it has no default investment at all. Money you put in sits as cash until you actively invest it, earning 0.05% a year at the three agent banks (DBS/POSB, OCBC and UOB). An analysis of Ministry of Finance data puts the idle amount at roughly S$5.0 billion in December 2025, 21% of the S$23.88 billion contributed, up from 19% (S$3.9bn of S$20.58bn) in December 2024 (SmartWealth analysis of MOF data, https://www.smartwealth.sg/; DBS/POSB back up the December 2024 figure, https://www.posb.com.sg/personal/articles/retirement/grow-your-srs-savings). The defaults research predicts exactly this. If doing nothing leaves your money in cash, a lot of people will do nothing. No academic study has yet looked at uninvested cash in a voluntary retirement scheme like SRS.

## Recommendations
1. **State the SRS academic void confidently but precisely.** Use the formulation "no peer-reviewed study takes the SRS as its primary subject," naming the databases searched (Google Scholar, SSRN, RePEc, NUS ScholarBank, SMU InK, NTU DR-NTU, *Singapore Economic Review*, *JPEF*) and citing the MOF admission that research is nascent. This is the paper's strongest hook. *Threshold that would change this:* if a dedicated SRS peer-reviewed article or an NUS/SMU thesis is later found, downgrade the claim from "absent" to "nascent/thin."
2. **Anchor the model in Dammon–Spatt–Zhang (2004) + Horan (2006) + Brown–Cederburg–O'Doherty (2017)**, then differentiate on three axes no existing paper covers jointly: (a) the **no-CGT decomposition** isolating rate arbitrage (Stream 8); (b) **flat fractional 50% inclusion** (Stream 7); (c) a **finite forced-liquidation window** rather than an RMD floor (Stream 4).
3. **Lead the international section with the taxonomy gap.** Present India (60% exempt) and the UK (25% exempt) as partial analogues, and argue the OECD EET/TEE taxonomy has no clean cell for Singapore's flat marginal-rate 50% inclusion. Note Singapore's absence from OECD datasets as a framing device, not a weakness.
4. **Develop the real-options sub-model as a distinct, high-upside contribution** (Stream 6): the cost of breaking the lock-up is known and bounded (5% plus half the marginal rate, at most about 17%), which makes the option unusually tractable, and it is unstudied.
5. **Use the idle-cash statistic as the empirical motivation for a behavioural section** (Stream 10), tying the SRS's lack of a default investment option to Madrian–Shea. *Before publication, re-verify the ~21%/S$5bn figure against a primary MOF/MAS dataset* rather than the SmartWealth secondary analysis.

## Caveats
- **Paywalls limit exhaustiveness.** ProQuest, Cambridge Core, and JSTOR could not be fully text-searched; the SRS-absence claim is high-confidence but not provably exhaustive. Keep a documented search log to defend it.
- **Several SRS-descriptive sources are commercial** (banks, robo-advisers such as Endowus/StashAway/DBS/OCBC) and are authoritative only for scheme mechanics, not for behaviour or magnitudes.
- **The idle-cash figure (≈21%, ≈S$5bn)** traces to a secondary analysis of MOF data (SmartWealth), corroborated for 2024 by DBS/POSB; verify against a primary regulator dataset before publication.
- **Corrected contributor statistics:** the S$9,000 average contribution and 3.2% average marginal rate apply to the *minority* (10%) of SRS contributors earning below S$80,000; the *majority* (90%) earn above S$80,000, contribute ~S$14,000, and face ~15% marginal rates (MOF Parliamentary Reply, 2019). Use the corrected figures.
- **Two SRS-mentioning journals (David Publishing; IISTE) are predatory** and must not be cited.
- **Amador, Werning & Angeletos (2006)** and the primary text of the 2025 published *JFE* "Optimal Illiquidity" should be verified directly from the journals rather than via citing papers before final submission.
- The **2025 MPC arXiv preprint** (Stream 4) is not peer-reviewed; cite as a working paper.
