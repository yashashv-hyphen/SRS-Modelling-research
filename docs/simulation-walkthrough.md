# Does SRS make you richer? — a plain-language walkthrough

Generated by `srs_simulation.py`. It plays out many possible futures for the same person — the market rising and falling each year, and the tax rules possibly drifting — then reports the middle outcome and the range. It is **not** advice or a forecast; it stress-tests the model in `srs_model.py`. SRS is Singapore's optional retirement account: you get a tax discount for paying in, and pay tax on only half of what you take out later.

## The bottom line

For someone with **$160,000** taxable income, using SRS leaves them about **$177k richer** by the end than if they had simply invested the same out-of-pocket money in a normal account — so it is **worth it ✅**

Depending on how markets and taxes turn out, that head start lands somewhere between **$77k** (unlucky) and **$379k** (lucky), across 200 simulated lifetimes. Both paths start from the exact same amount of your own cash — the tax discount is the extra money SRS lets you invest.

## 1. With SRS vs. without

| | Money at the end (middle outcome) | Unlucky → lucky |
|---|---|---|
| **With SRS** | $1.04M | $451k → $2.37M |
| **Without SRS** (invest normally) | $874k | $373k → $1.99M |

## 2. Should you keep putting money in?

Two ways to look at the **next dollar** you put in, in the first year:

- **Right now (the marginal view):** you save about **15¢** of tax on that dollar today.
- **Later:** you'll pay about **3.5¢** of tax when you take it out.
- Since 15¢ saved beats 3.5¢ paid, the next dollar is **worth putting in**.

Adding more only stops being worth it once your SRS pot would grow past roughly **$1.85M** (where the tax you'd pay taking it out finally catches up with the discount going in). The middle simulated pot here reaches **$814k** — well under that line.

Across all simulated lifetimes at this income, a tax shock made a dollar not-worth-it at some point in **0.0%** of them.

## 3. Taking the money out: the smart way pays less tax

The model takes the money out with a **smart plan** that re-decides each year how much to withdraw, based on how your investments actually did (this is the "MPC" strategy). Compared with the popular **"take $40,000 a year"** rule of thumb, the smart plan pays about **$30k less tax** in the middle outcome (range -$126 → $139k). The gap is small when tax brackets sit still and grows when a tax shock lands mid-withdrawal.

## 4. After the money is out: spend it, or leave it?

With the **$1.08M** you end up holding, two honest choices:

- **Spend it evenly** so it runs out at the assumed age of death: about **$121k a year** to spend, nothing left for family.
- **Never touch it** and leave it to family: about **$1.93M** left behind.

## 5. Breaking out early: how good must an outside deal be?

Say a great opportunity comes along partway through (year 15) and you're tempted to pull your money out early, paying the 5% penalty and full tax. To come out ahead, that outside opportunity would have to earn more than **6.9% every year** until you'd have been allowed to withdraw anyway. For scale: a fixed deposit pays ~3% and the stock market averages ~7%, so this only makes sense for a genuinely exceptional deal. The hurdle climbs the closer you are to being allowed to withdraw.

## 7. How the market changes the answer

The dollar head start grows with the market, but the *percentage* edge shrinks (the tax-free half covers less of a bigger pot).

| Average market return | Head start with SRS (middle) | As a % |
|---|---|---|
| 3% a year | $96k | 17.3% |
| 4% a year | $119k | 16.8% |
| 5% a year | $147k | 16.2% |
| 6% a year | $180k | 15.6% |
| 7% a year | $225k | 14.9% |
| 8% a year | $275k | 14.0% |

## 8. How the tax future changes the answer

Three futures for the tax bands across the contributing years. Bracket creep actually *raises* the advantage — a rising salary drifts into higher bands, so a bigger discount goes in.

| Tax future | Head start with SRS (middle) | Unlucky → lucky |
|---|---|---|
| Brackets frozen forever | $109k | $49k → $242k |
| Bracket creep (pay rises) | $147k | $67k → $337k |
| Creep + rate-change shocks | $147k | $64k → $302k |

## What this does *not* model

Mortality risk (age at death is a fixed input, not a probability), the annuity clause, the foreigner withholding concession, other retirement income, and the $80,000 overall personal-relief cap. The tax-drift model is illustrative, not a calibrated forecast. See `docs/open-tasks.md` and the docstrings in `srs_simulation.py`.
